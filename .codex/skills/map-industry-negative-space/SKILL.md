# Map Industry Negative Space

Find opportunity in the relationships and routes that a market's dominant observers flatten, overlook, or cannot see from their current position. The goal is not a list of market gaps. It is an evidence-backed model of who misses what, why they miss it, how a route can be traversed, and what advantageous position lies at the end.

## Working Vocabulary

- **Positive space:** visible competitors, products, categories, customers, prices, channels, metrics, regulations, and accepted value chains.
- **Negative space:** relationships, seams, handoffs, combinations, intervals, and background behavior that are observable in principle but do not receive sustained attention.
- **Dead space:** activity the focal observer cannot currently see because of its data, incentives, organizational boundaries, channel position, category definitions, geography, time horizon, or measurement system.
- **Surface:** an incumbent strength, economic barrier, regulatory constraint, crowded position, or capability mismatch.
- **Gap:** an observer-relative weakness or seam that a particular entrant can potentially traverse.
- **Avenue of approach:** a chain of two or more gaps that allows an entrant to learn, enter, or build position before provoking a full response.
- **Position:** a defensible source of access, distribution, trust, data, cost advantage, workflow control, or customer value reached through the route.
- **Reveal threshold:** the point at which the move becomes legible enough for incumbents, regulators, customers, or complements to respond.

Negative space is overlooked; dead space is inaccessible from the present vantage. Do not call the absence of evidence a hidden opportunity.

## Scope And Evidence

Define the niche, geography, customer or user, value chain, time horizon, and decision the map should inform. Infer reasonable boundaries when the request is exploratory and label them. Ask a focused question only when two plausible scopes would produce materially different maps.

Choose a **focal observer** rather than treating "the industry" as one mind. Relevant observers can include dominant incumbents, a specific customer group, suppliers, distributors, platforms, regulators, analysts, or investors. Map additional observers only when their different lines of sight create an important seam.

When external facts matter and research tools are available, verify current products, pricing, market structure, regulations, customer behavior, and business models. Prefer primary sources, direct market evidence, and credible research. Label sourced fact, reported behavior, inference, and hypothesis. If research is unavailable, explicitly treat the map as provisional.

## Run The Sequence

1. **Map the terrain.** Describe the visible market: actors, offers, customer groups, substitutes, complements, channels, value flow, common metrics, rules, and category boundaries.
2. **Model the observer.** Reconstruct what the focal observer monitors and rewards: current revenue, margin thresholds, major accounts, benchmark features, analyst categories, procurement rules, utilization, regulatory definitions, planning cycles, and organizational ownership. Explain how this attention system shapes the observer's operational picture.
3. **Map positive space.** Show the obvious competitive positions and narratives. Note which dimensions absorb disproportionate attention and which actors define the language of the category.
4. **Locate negative space.** Inspect nonconsumption and workarounds; overserved or low-status users; unowned handoffs; cross-category jobs; missing complements; mismatched time scales; unused by-products; information discontinuities; geographic, regulatory, or channel seams; and needs expressed in language the category does not recognize.
5. **Locate dead space.** Identify what the observer's data, economics, structure, or worldview prevents it from seeing. For every proposed dead space, state the occluding mechanism and the new vantage or evidence needed to inspect it.
6. **Identify surfaces and gaps.** Mark incumbent strengths and response zones. Then show which weaknesses are gaps only for an entrant with a particular capability, cost structure, trust position, technology, distribution path, or time horizon.
7. **Link avenues of approach.** Construct two or three routes from at least two connected gaps. For each route specify the entry point, overlooked customer behavior or seam, sequence of moves, required capability, learning gained at each step, end position, reveal threshold, likely response, and reason the route may remain open long enough.
8. **Evaluate the position.** Compare routes using evidence rather than faux precision: customer value, observer-specific blindness, traversability, capability fit, economics, speed of learning, time to reveal, response latency, defensibility, legal constraints, and ethical effects.
9. **Red-team the thesis.** Seek reasons the space is unoccupied, evidence incumbents already see it, hidden dependencies, adoption barriers, adverse selection, regulatory failure, and ways a direct response or copy could collapse the advantage.
10. **Design probes.** Recommend the smallest interviews, landing pages, concierge tests, data analyses, partnership conversations, or prototypes that discriminate among the leading routes. State what evidence would support, revise, or kill each thesis.

## Deliver The Map

Lead with the highest-confidence avenue of approach and the causal reason it remains under-observed. Then provide:

- **Scope and focal observer**
- **Attention map:** what the observer foregrounds, measures, rewards, and excludes
- **Space map:** negative space, dead space, occluding mechanism, evidence, and confidence
- **Surfaces and gaps:** including why each gap is entrant-specific
- **Avenues of approach:** route, capabilities, ending position, reveal threshold, and likely response
- **Best probes:** ordered by information gained per unit of time or cost
- **Contrary evidence and unknowns**

Do not equate low competition with opportunity, a customer complaint with willingness to pay, or novelty with defensibility. A credible route must identify the observer, the cause of blindness, a traversable sequence, an end position with value, and a test that could prove the thesis wrong.
