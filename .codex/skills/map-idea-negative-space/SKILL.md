# Map Idea Negative Space

Expose what an idea's current framing makes difficult to notice, then connect the strongest omissions into more powerful formulations, implications, or tests. Preserve the original kernel before transforming it. The goal is not associative brainstorming; every connection must change the explanation, prediction, design, or action.

## Working Vocabulary

- **Positive space:** the idea's explicit claims, named entities, metaphor, causal story, evidence, intended audience, and visible application.
- **Negative space:** omitted relationships, contrasts, intervals, background conditions, and implications that are available in principle but receive little attention because of the current framing.
- **Dead space:** questions the idea cannot answer from its present concepts or evidence.
- **Surface:** a contradiction, boundary condition, entrenched frame, evidentiary limit, or mechanism the idea cannot pass through unchanged.
- **Gap:** an observer-relative opening created by an unstated assumption, missing relation, unexplored scale, or neglected point of view.
- **Avenue of approach:** a chain of two or more gaps that leads to a stronger thesis, product, research question, narrative, or practical application.
- **Position:** the new explanatory leverage, differentiation, prediction, or design possibility reached through the route.
- **Reveal threshold:** the observation, formulation, or demonstration that makes the new connection obvious to others and invites criticism, adoption, or imitation.

Negative space is unattended content; dead space requires a new concept, source, experiment, or vantage. Do not fill dead space with confident invention.

## Establish The Observer Set

An idea does not have negative space in the abstract; it has negative space relative to an observer and framing. Model the perspectives that matter:

- the author or originator;
- the intended audience or user;
- the strongest informed critic;
- an implementer affected by practical constraints;
- an adjacent discipline when it offers a genuinely different unit of analysis.

Use only the perspectives that can materially change the idea. If the intended purpose is unclear, infer a working purpose and label it; ask one focused question only when the difference between explanation, product, research, and persuasion would change the analysis.

When originality, precedent, or current applicability matters and research is available, look for prior concepts and disconfirming evidence. Distinguish documented precedent, analogy, inference, and original synthesis.

## Run The Sequence

1. **Protect the kernel.** Restate the idea's smallest consequential insight in its strongest form. Separate the kernel from its current metaphor, examples, conclusions, and rhetoric so exploration does not accidentally erase it.
2. **Map positive space.** List the explicit actors, objects, claims, mechanism, scale, time horizon, unit of analysis, evidence, desired outcome, and implied audience. Diagram relationships when the idea has three or more interacting parts.
3. **Model attention.** Identify what the framing makes salient: the protagonist, metaphor, familiar category, moral valence, visible outcome, named objects, chosen metric, and level of analysis. Explain what each observer is therefore likely to notice or ignore.
4. **Reverse figure and ground.** Make the relationships, absences, constraints, handoffs, background conditions, and intervals primary. Ask what changes when the idea is viewed from the recipient, excluded actor, system, edge, or environment rather than the named figure.
5. **Search the negative space.** Use the lenses that fit the idea: missing relationship, absent stakeholder, inverse claim, counterfactual, unspoken mechanism, boundary condition, different time scale, different level of analysis, measurement blind spot, second-order effect, latent complement, adjacent-domain analogue, or conflict between local and system incentives.
6. **Mark dead space.** Identify questions the current concepts or evidence cannot resolve. State what new distinction, source, observation, experiment, comparison, or vocabulary would change the vantage.
7. **Identify surfaces and gaps.** Show where the idea breaks, becomes conventional, lacks causal support, or meets a strong objection. Then identify openings that exist for this framing but not for competing explanations.
8. **Link avenues of approach.** Combine two or more gaps into two or three consequential routes. A route must end in a stronger claim, novel prediction, product mechanism, research program, narrative frame, or action—not merely another association.
9. **Test the new position.** For each route, articulate the mechanism, what becomes newly explainable or possible, the reveal threshold, strongest precedent, strongest objection, falsifying observation, and practical consequence if true.
10. **Refine without inflating.** Produce the shortest upgraded articulation that preserves the kernel and incorporates only the negative-space findings that survived testing.

## Deliver The Map

Lead with the most consequential thing the original framing leaves unseen. Then provide:

- **Original kernel:** the idea worth protecting
- **Observer and attention map:** who sees what, through which frame
- **Positive-space model:** explicit elements and relationships
- **Negative-space findings:** omission, observer, cause, consequence, and confidence
- **Dead-space questions:** what cannot yet be known and how to change the vantage
- **Avenues of approach:** connected gaps, ending positions, and reveal thresholds
- **Refined idea:** a compact upgraded formulation
- **Decisive tests:** observations or experiments that could strengthen or kill it

Reject association soup, renaming without explanatory gain, and claims of originality based only on unfamiliar wording. A valuable negative-space finding must identify the omitted relation, explain why the framing hides it, and show how recovering it changes what can be understood, predicted, built, or done.
