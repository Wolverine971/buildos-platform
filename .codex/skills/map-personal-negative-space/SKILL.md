# Map Personal Negative Space

Find valuable action paths that the person or consequential outside observers do not naturally see. Treat this as observer-relative systems analysis, not generic self-improvement or personality diagnosis.

## Working Vocabulary

- **Positive space:** visible labels, roles, credentials, skills, projects, relationships, goals, and conventional options.
- **Negative space:** relationships, combinations, intervals, and background patterns that are visible in principle but routinely unattended.
- **Dead space:** information or opportunity that cannot be seen from the current position because evidence, exposure, feedback, language, access, or a useful comparison is missing.
- **Surface:** a hard spot such as a binding constraint, crowded path, credibility barrier, or incentive working against movement.
- **Gap:** an observer-relative seam or weakness that this person can potentially traverse.
- **Avenue of approach:** two or more linked gaps that form a credible sequence of actions.
- **Position:** the leverage, access, credibility, learning, or option created at the end of a route.
- **Reveal threshold:** the event after which other people recognize the move and can support, copy, block, or compete with it.

Negative space is overlooked; dead space is not currently knowable. Do not present a dead-space guess as an insight. State what new vantage, evidence, or experiment could reveal it.

## Establish The Two-Sided Observer Model

Analyze the field from both sides:

1. **The person as observer:** what their goals, habits, identity, metrics, peer group, past experience, and current pressures cause them to foreground or ignore.
2. **The outside observer:** what a relevant employer, customer, collaborator, audience, institution, or community can and cannot recognize about the person.

Choose the one to three outside observers that materially affect the stated objective. Do not substitute a vague "market" or "everyone" for an identifiable observer. If the objective or relevant observer cannot be inferred, ask one focused question; otherwise proceed with explicit assumptions.

Separate user-provided facts, externally verified facts, inference, and unknowns. Do not infer sensitive traits, private relationships, mental states, or clinical explanations.

## Run The Sequence

1. **Define the field.** State the person's objective, relevant time horizon, constraints, available resources, and the decision this analysis should improve.
2. **Map positive space.** Inventory the obvious story: roles, skills, credentials, projects, assets, relationships, audiences, and conventional next moves. Capture how the person currently describes themselves and how outsiders are likely to categorize them.
3. **Model attention.** Identify what organizes each observer's attention: incentives, status markers, KPIs, search terms, social comparisons, familiar categories, feedback sources, urgent pressures, and inherited assumptions. Explain the causal reason for each claimed blind spot.
4. **Map negative space.** Look especially for unusual skill combinations, repeated patterns across unrelated experiences, credibility in two worlds, underused access, dormant relationships, unowned handoffs, mismatches between formal title and actual capability, and assets created as by-products of prior work.
5. **Mark dead space.** Identify consequential unknowns caused by missing feedback, missing exposure, inaccessible data, untested capability, or a one-sided network. Convert each into a question, conversation, observation, or small probe that changes the vantage point.
6. **Identify surfaces and gaps.** Distinguish genuine constraints from conventions. A path can be a surface for the average person but a gap for this person because of their particular experience, trust, access, timing, or cost structure.
7. **Link avenues of approach.** Build two or three routes from at least two connected gaps. For each, specify the entry action, why it is under-attended, the sequence, needed capabilities, likely friction, resulting position, reveal threshold, and how the environment may respond.
8. **Red-team the routes.** Test whether the space is empty because it lacks value, whether the claimed connection is causal or merely clever, whether the person truly has an advantage, and whether the route depends on deception, exploitation, or assumptions about another person's private state.
9. **Choose a probe.** Recommend the smallest action that produces decision-quality evidence. Favor reversible demonstrations, conversations, prototypes, or public artifacts over a large commitment.

## Deliver The Map

Lead with the strongest newly visible opportunity and why this person is unusually positioned to pursue it. Then provide:

- **Kernel:** the person's distinctive but currently underused source of leverage.
- **Observer map:** what the person and outside observers foreground, reward, and miss.
- **Space map:** each negative or dead space, who misses it, why, supporting evidence, and confidence.
- **Avenues of approach:** the connected routes, ending positions, reveal thresholds, and likely responses.
- **Best probe:** one concrete next action, what it tests, and what result would support or kill the route.
- **Unknowns:** what cannot responsibly be concluded from the current vantage.

Avoid advice that could apply to almost anyone. Do not reduce the result to "network more," "build a personal brand," or "combine your skills" without naming the exact relationship, observer, mechanism, and test. Being overlooked can create room to move; it is not permission to manipulate people or misrepresent intent.
